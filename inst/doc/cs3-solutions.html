  1. Yes. Death with a functioning graft precludes subsequent graft failure. Ending follow-up at death is appropriate for estimating the cause-specific hazard among patients who are alive with a functioning graft. For a cumulative incidence analysis, coding death as censoring leaves it out of the set of competing events. The resulting estimates do not measure the probability of graft failure before death.

To estimate that probability, record death with a functioning graft as a fifth event type and keep the four types of graft failure as the outcomes of interest. Adding the five cumulative incidences would answer a separate question about the probability of graft failure or death. The distributed data do not separately identify the deaths with a functioning graft, so we cannot estimate the five-event version from this file.

  1. The cause-specific cumulative incidence function \(F_j(t) = P(T \le t, C = j)\) gives the probability of failure from cause \(j\) by time \(t\) in the presence of the competing causes. It can be estimated over the observed follow-up when the competing events are recorded and the usual independent censoring assumption holds. Its limit as \(t\) increases is the probability that cause \(j\) ever occurs.

The cause-specific survivor function \(S_j^\dagger(t)\) is derived from the hazard of cause \(j\) alone. Interpreting it as the survivor function if all other causes were removed assumes that removing them would leave the hazard of cause \(j\) unchanged.

  1. The Kaplan–Meier estimate of the overall survivor function uses the event and censoring times regardless of the cause labels. Reassigning events to or from cause \(j\) can change its cumulative incidence function. Splitting a competing cause into subtypes preserves both the events of type \(j\) and the overall survivor function, so the cumulative incidence of cause \(j\) stays the same.

Solution 2

  1. True. Because the causes are mutually exclusive, the probability of some event in a short interval is the sum over causes, and the hazards add: \(h(t) = \sum_j h_j(t)\), hence \(S(t) = \exp\{-\sum_j H_j(t)\}\).

  2. True. The joint distribution of \((T_1, \dots, T_m)\) is not identifiable from competing risks data. For an uncensored observation, we see the earliest failure time and its cause.

  3. False. If all cause-specific hazards are unchanged, then so is \(S(t)\), and hence every cumulative incidence function. The cumulative incidence of cause \(j\) is \(F_j(t) = \int_0^t h_j(u)\,S(u)\,du\), and \(S(u)\) contains the hazards of all causes. A variable can leave \(h_j\) alone and still change \(F_j\) by changing the hazard of a competitor.

  1. \(1 - \hat S_j(t)\) estimates the complement of the cause-specific survivor function, \(1 - S_j^\dagger(t) = 1 - \exp\{-H_j(t)\}\).   2. Because \(F_j(t) = \int_0^t h_j(u)\,S(u)\,du\), and \(S(u)\) is the survivor function of all causes pooled. Both estimators use the same risk set just before a given failure time. In Table 12.4 each cause-\(j\) event fraction is weighted by the estimated probability of still being event-free just before that time. The cause-specific Kaplan–Meier product uses only the cause-\(j\) event fractions in its factors.

  1. The subhazard is the derivative of the cumulative incidence function divided by \(1 - F_j(t)\). The denominator is the probability of still being event-free or of having already had a competing graft failure. In the fit, individuals retained after a competing event are weighted to account for censoring. The subhazard ratio compares PBC with ALD at the same age and gender. Under the fitted proportional subhazards model, a ratio greater than 1 gives a higher predicted cumulative incidence for PBC when the baseline cumulative incidence is positive and below 1.

Solution 12

  1. The subhazard at time \(t\) equals \(h_j(t)S(t)/\{1-F_j(t)\}\). The two ratios can differ because the groups also differ in their overall survivor functions and in the fraction that has already died from a competing cause. Many standard-group mice die early from other causes. They leave the cause-specific risk set but remain in the subdistribution risk set. The two fits therefore use different risk sets over time. Moreover, the constant Fine and Gray ratio for reticulum cell sarcoma is only a summary because the comparison varies with time, as shown in Question 13.

  2. The fitted constant subhazard ratio near 1 can hide differences over time. The cumulative incidence of death from other causes starts out lower in the germ-free group and ends at 46.3 %, compared with 39.4 % in the standard group. This can happen alongside a lower estimated cause-specific hazard: the cumulative incidence function depends on both \(h_j(t)\) and the overall survivor function \(S(t)\). A larger \(S(t)\) means more mice remain at risk of dying from that cause later.

  3. With complete follow-up until death, the estimated ultimate probabilities answer the question directly. The proportion dying from reticulum cell sarcoma was 38.4 % in the standard group and 18.3 % in the germ-free group. If a model is needed, a Fine and Gray model can be used to predict the cumulative incidence function for each group, allowing the subhazard ratio to vary with time as shown in Question 13.

  4. No. Gray’s test compares the cumulative incidence functions over follow-up without assuming proportional subhazards. The Fine and Gray model in Question 11(b) summarises the comparison with a single constant ratio. That ratio can be close to 1 when the groups differ in opposite directions at different times. Question 13 checks this explanation.

q13a

With proportional subhazards, we expect the smooth to fluctuate around zero without a systematic trend. Here it is negative early and rises above zero later, suggesting a time-varying association. The plot uses unscaled Schoenfeld-type residuals against time in days. It is analogous to Collett’s diagnostic in Figure 12.4, which uses scaled residuals.
