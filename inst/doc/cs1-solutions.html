1. From a clinical question to a time-to-event data set

A survival analysis starts with four decisions: the time origin, the time scale, the event and the censoring mechanism. These decisions are part of the estimand, not a technical detail. The help pages (?iud, ?breast, ?prostate, ?melanoma, ?pulmonary, and ?sta310r::myeloma) give the background of each study.

1. For each (or some) of the six data sets, identify

  1. the time origin;
  2. the time scale and its unit;
  3. the event of interest;
  4. what a censored observation means, and whether the censoring is administrative, due to loss to follow-up, or due to a competing event.

Some help pages state the origin explicitly; where they do not, say what you would assume and why. You may answer in a table. Which data set has no censoring at all?

Solution 1.

  • pulmonary (11 men, no censoring): time origin is not stated explicitly in the example, but is presumably diagnosis or the start of treatment of the pulmonary metastasis; time scale is months; event is death.

  • iud (18 women): time origin is the first day of IUD use; time scale is weeks; event is discontinuation because of menstrual bleeding. The 9 censored observations are women who stopped for another reason, were lost to follow-up, or reached the two-year protocol limit.

  • breast (45 women): time origin is surgery (mastectomy); time scale is months; event is death from breast cancer. The 19 censored observations are women alive in July 1987, women who died from other causes, and losses to follow-up.

  • myeloma (48 patients): time origin is diagnosis; time scale is months; event is death from multiple myeloma. 12 observations are censored at the end of the study.

  • prostate (38 men): time origin is randomisation; time scale is months; event is death from prostatic cancer. The 32 censored observations are deaths from other causes, losses to follow-up, and administrative censoring at the end of follow-up.

  • melanoma (30 patients): time origin is surgical removal of the tumour followed by randomisation; time scale is months; event is death. The 20 censored observations are patients alive at the end of follow-up.

  • No censoring: pulmonary.

  • Median not estimable: prostate and melanoma; in breast, the median is estimable overall (113 months) but not in the HPA-negative subgroup.

  • A censored observation at the largest time: iud, breast, prostate and melanoma. In iud the largest time is 107 weeks, where there are 1 event and 2 censorings; myeloma has its largest observation at 91 months, which is a death.

3. True or false?

  1. A patient censored at 10 months is still in the risk set at 12 months.
  2. If censoring is informative, the Kaplan–Meier estimate is always biased upwards.
  3. The hazard \(h(t)\) is the probability of dying at time \(t\).
  4. Administrative censoring at the end of a study is usually independent censoring, provided the analysis time was specified before the data were examined.
  5. Left censoring means that the true event time is smaller than the observed time, and interval censoring means that the event time is known only to lie in an interval.
  6. Study time is calendar time in the study, while patient time is measured from each patient’s own time origin. Administrative censoring at a fixed calendar date therefore produces different patient-time censoring times for patients recruited at different dates.

Solution 3.

  1. False. A woman censored at 10 months contributes to the risk set up to 10 months and is removed immediately after 10 months. She is not at risk at 12 months.

  2. False. Informative censoring can bias the estimate in either direction. If high-risk patients leave, survival is overestimated; if low-risk patients leave, survival is underestimated.

  3. False. The hazard is an instantaneous rate, not a probability. It is the limiting probability of an event in a short interval divided by the length of that interval, and it can be greater than 1.

  4. True, provided the analysis time was specified in advance and the censoring is not related to prognosis. Administrative censoring at a pre-specified calendar date is the usual example of independent censoring.

  5. True.

  6. True.. Study time is calendar time measured from the start of the study; patient time is measured from each patient’s own time origin. If a study closes on a fixed calendar date, a patient recruited late has less patient time at closure than a patient recruited early. Administrative censoring therefore gives different patient-time censoring times, even though all patients are censored on the same calendar date. This is usually independent censoring if the closing date was pre-specified and the recruitment date is unrelated to prognosis.

2. The empirical survivor function when there is no censoring

The pulmonary data contain the survival times of 11 men with pulmonary metastasis from osteosarcoma, with no censoring. The empirical survivor function is simply the proportion of men whose survival time is at least \(t\): \[\hat S(t) = \frac{\text{number with survival time} \ge t}{n}.\] The estimated curve is a step function, constant between adjacent death times and decreasing at each death time.

4. Interpret the table:

emp_pulm <- pulmonary |>
  distinct(time) |>
  arrange(time) |>
  mutate(
    n_at_least = map_int(time, \(t) sum(pulmonary$time >= t)),
    S_hat = n_at_least / nrow(pulmonary)
  )

Is S_hat equivalemnt to the survival column from summary(survfit(Surv(pulmonary$time) ~ 1))? If not, why?

Plot \(\hat S(t)\) above as a step function (HINT: ggplot2::geom_step()) and compare with:

With the empirical convention in the table, the estimate is 1.000 at 11 months and drops at 13, 14, 15, 17 months; the sizes of those drops are 0.091, 0.455, 0.182, 0.182. It is flat between 15 and 17 months because no one dies in that interval.

There is a small convention issue at an event time. The empirical definition \(P(T\ge t)\) gives an estimate of 1.000 at 11 months, because all 11 men have a survival time of at least 11 months. survfit() reports 0.909 at 11 months, because it lets the curve drop at the event time and reports the value after the drop. Between event times the two conventions agree, and at 12 months both give 0.909. The important practical point is to say whether a reported “survival at 12 months” includes deaths at exactly 12 months.

5. The observed times are

11, 13, 13, 13, 13, 13, 14, 14, 15, 15, 17 months.

Their mean is 13.7272727 months and their median is 13 months. Why is the median usually preferred for survival data? Does the mean or the median describe the “typical” patient better here? What happens to the mean if the largest observation is censored?

Solution 5.

The mean is 13.73 months and the median is 13.00 months. Survival times are usually right-skewed and the mean is sensitive to a few long survivors; the median is the time by which half the patients have died and is more robust. Here the mean is slightly larger than the median, which is consistent with a right tail. If the largest observation were censored, the mean would not be estimable without an assumption about the unobserved tail; treating the censored time as a death would underestimate the mean. The median can still be estimated if the curve reaches 0.5.

6. Five men die at 13 months. With the convention that the curve drops at the event time (the convention used by survfit()), the estimated survival is 0.909 just before 13 months and 0.455 at 13 months. If one of the men had been censored at exactly 13 months instead of dying, should that censored observation be counted before or after the deaths at 13 months when the risk set is formed? What difference would the other convention make to the estimate? (Collett discusses this in Section 2.1.2.)

Solution 6.

The censored observation should be counted after the deaths at 13 months: the risk set just before that time includes everyone still under observation at that time, including the person who is censored there. The deaths are then removed, and the censored observation is removed afterwards. If the censoring were counted before the deaths, the risk set would be too small and the estimated survival would change.

7. (Optional.) When there is no censoring, Greenwood’s formula reduces to the binomial standard error \[\operatorname{se}\{\hat S(t)\} = \sqrt{\frac{\hat S(t)(1-\hat S(t))}{n}}.\]

  1. Use this formula to compute the standard error of \(\hat S(13)\) and compare it with the std.err reported by survfit.

  2. Why is the standard error NaN at the last death time, where \(\hat S(t)=0\)? Does that mean the estimate is known with certainty?

Solution 7 (Optional).

  1. After 13 months, 6 of the 11 men have died, so \(\hat S(13)=0.4545\). The binomial standard error is

\[\operatorname{se}\{\hat S(13)\} = \sqrt{\frac{0.4545 \times 0.5455}{11}} = 0.1501.\] This is the std.err reported by survfit() at 13 months.

  1. At the last death time there is one person at risk and that person dies, so \(\hat S(t)=0\). Greenwood’s sum contains the term \(d/(n(n-d))=1/(1\times 0)=\infty\), and \(0\times\infty\) is NaN. The estimate is zero because the only remaining person died, but that does not mean the survival probability is known with certainty; the sample is small and the tail is poorly estimated. This is one reason why the last part of a Kaplan–Meier curve is fragile.

3. Hazard and cumulative hazard

8. The cumulative hazard is \(H(t)=-\log S(t)\), and the Nelson–Aalen estimate is the cumulative sum of the event fractions \(d_j/n_j\). The cumhaz component of a survfit object is the Nelson–Aalen estimate. Compare it with \(-\log \hat S(t)\) from the Kaplan–Meier estimate:

km_myeloma <- survfit(Surv(time, status) ~ 1, data = sta310r::myeloma)
  1. Which cumulative hazard is larger, the Nelson–Aalen estimate or \(-\log\hat S(t)\)? Is that guaranteed, or does it depend on the data?
  2. What does the slope of the cumulative hazard curve represent?
  3. The two estimates of \(S(t)\) differ by only a few percentage points. Is the difference practically important compared with the width of the confidence intervals?

Solution 8.

ch <- km_myeloma |>
  summary(times = c(24, 48, 60), extend = TRUE) |>
  with(tibble::tibble(
    time = time,
    nelson_aalen = cumhaz,
    minus_log_km = -log(surv),
    S_from_na = exp(-cumhaz),
    S_km = surv
  ))

knitr::kable(ch, digits = 3)
time nelson_aalen minus_log_km S_from_na S_km
24 0.942 0.979 0.390 0.376
48 1.186 1.242 0.306 0.289
60 1.422 1.493 0.241 0.225

At 24 months the Nelson–Aalen cumulative hazard is 0.942 and \(-\log\hat S(t)\) is 0.979. At 48 months they are 1.186 and 1.242; at 60 months 1.422 and 1.493. The Kaplan–Meier-based value is larger. This is guaranteed: for \(0<x<1\), \(-\log(1-x)>x\), so \(-\log\hat S_{KM}(t)=\sum_j-\log(1-d_j/n_j)>\sum_j d_j/n_j=\hat H_{NA}(t)\). Consequently the Nelson–Aalen survivor estimate \(\exp(-\hat H_{NA})\) is larger than the Kaplan–Meier estimate.

  1. The slope of the cumulative hazard curve is the hazard: a steeper slope means a higher event rate.

summary(km_myeloma, c(24, 48, 60))
#> Call: survfit(formula = Surv(time, status) ~ 1, data = sta310r::myeloma)
#> 
#>  time n.risk n.event survival std.err lower 95% CI upper 95% CI
#>    24     14      27    0.376  0.0762        0.252        0.559
#>    48      9       3    0.289  0.0732        0.176        0.475
#>    60      5       2    0.225  0.0696        0.122        0.412

The two estimates of \(S(t)\) differ by about 0.04 in cumulative hazard at 24 months, which corresponds to survival estimates of 0.390 versus 0.376. The pointwise confidence interval at 24 months is much wider: 0.376 with 95 % interval 0.252–0.559. The difference between the estimators is therefore not practically important compared with the statistical uncertainty.

Solution 10.

  1. The largest observed time is for:
slice_max(iud, time)
#>   time status

The Kaplan–Meier estimate is stays constant from week 97, until just before week 107. It is not defined after 107 weeks, because there is no one left at risk after the event and the censorings at week 107.

  1. If all observations at week 107 had been censored, there would be no event, so the estimate would remain from week 97, and it would still be undefined after week 107. Continuing the curve flat would assume that no events occur after the end of follow-up, which is an extra assumption, not a result of the data. The curve therefore stops at the last observed time.

12. A colleague ignores censoring and estimates 2-year survival in the iud data as “the proportion of women who did not discontinue within 104 weeks”, i.e. \(1-9/18=0.50\).

  1. Compare this with the Kaplan–Meier estimate \(\hat S(104)\).
  2. In which direction is the naive estimate biased, and why?
  3. Would the problem be worse or less serious in a study with 80 % censoring?
  4. Write one sentence interpreting \(\hat S(104)=0.373\) correctly.

Solution 12.

  1. The naive estimate is 0.5; the Kaplan–Meier estimate is 0.37. The naive estimate is too high by about 13 percentage points.

  2. The naive calculation treats the 9 censored women as if they had not discontinued during the full 104 weeks. In fact, they were only followed until their censoring time, and some of them would have discontinued later. Ignoring that incomplete follow-up inflates the estimated survival.

  3. The problem would be worse with 80 % censoring: more of the denominator would consist of women with incomplete follow-up treated as if they had completed the study. The bias would generally be larger.

  4. “The estimated probability that a woman is still using the IUD at 104 weeks (2 years) is 0.37, assuming that censoring is non-informative.” Or: “About 37 % of women are estimated to remain free of discontinuation due to bleeding at 2 years.”

7. Reporting and critical appraisal

21. Write a short results paragraph for the breast analysis (Questions above) that could be read by a clinical colleague.

Solution 21.

One adequate paragraph is:

Among 45 women with breast cancer, 26 died and 19 (42 %) were censored. Follow-up started at surgery, and the time scale was months. The Kaplan–Meier estimate of 5-year survival was 85 % (95 % CI 67 %–100 %) in the 13 women with HPA-negative tumours and 53 % (95 % CI 38 %–74 %) in the 32 women with HPA-positive tumours; the numbers at risk at 5 years were 11 and 17, respectively. Median survival was not reached in the negative group and was 64.5 months (95 % CI 41.0–not estimable) in the positive group. The log-rank test provided weak evidence of a difference (chi-square 3.5 on 1 degree of freedom, p = 0.061), with better survival in the HPA-negative group. Because the study is small and observational, these data do not establish that HPA staining causes a worse prognosis.

The exact wording can differ, but the paragraph should contain the numbers, the direction, the uncertainty and the limitation. It should not say “proves” or “causes”.

22. The following paragraph was written by a colleague. Find at least six problems and rewrite it.

“Among the 45 women, the mean survival time was 112 months. Women with positive HPA staining had a 2.5-fold higher hazard of death (log-rank p = 0.041), which proves that HPA staining causes metastasis. The p-value of 0.041 means that there is a 95.9 % probability that the null hypothesis is false. Because p < 0.05, the difference is clinically important. We also tested 20 subgroups and report the significant ones.”

Solution 22.

The paragraph contains at least the following problems:

  1. Mean survival is the wrong summary. Survival times are right-skewed and the mean is sensitive to the tail; with censoring, the mean is not even estimable without an assumption about the unobserved tail. Report the median or survival at fixed times.
  2. Censored women cannot be excluded. Excluding them treats incomplete follow-up as if those women had been followed to the end, and biases the estimate. Kaplan–Meier uses their follow-up up to censoring.
  3. The log-rank test does not estimate a hazard ratio. A “2.5-fold higher hazard” would come from a Cox model (Chapter 3), not from a log-rank test.
  4. “Proves that HPA staining causes metastasis” is causal language. The study is observational; association is not causation.
  5. The p-value is misinterpreted. A p-value of 0.041 is not the probability that the null hypothesis is false, and 95.9 % is not the posterior probability that the alternative is true. It is the probability of data at least as extreme as these, assuming the null hypothesis.
  6. Statistical significance is not clinical importance. A p-value below 0.05 does not by itself mean the difference is large or clinically meaningful; report the effect size and its confidence interval.
  7. The p-value quoted is not the log-rank p-value. The log-rank p-value is 0.041; 0.041 is closer to the Wilcoxon/Peto–Peto result. The test should be named correctly.
  8. Testing 20 subgroups and reporting the significant ones is selective reporting. This inflates the false-positive rate and makes the reported p-values unreliable. Pre-specify the primary analysis, report all pre-specified analyses, and treat subgroup findings as exploratory unless adjusted for multiplicity.

A corrected version might read:

Among 45 women, 26 died and 19 were censored. Kaplan–Meier estimates showed better survival in the HPA-negative group: 5-year survival was 85 % (95 % CI 67 %–100 %) versus 53 % (95 % CI 38 %–74 %). The log-rank test gave weak evidence of a difference (p = 0.061); the data are observational and do not establish causation. The analysis was pre-specified and no subgroup findings are reported as confirmatory.
