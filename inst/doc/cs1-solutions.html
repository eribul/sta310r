Solution 3

(Statements (b), (d), (e) and (f) revisit Lecture 1 (censoring, fixed vs random censoring, independent censoring, and study vs patient time); statement (c) is from Lecture 2 on the hazard.)

  1. False. A patient censored at 10 months contributes to the risk set up to 10 months and is removed immediately after 10 months. She is not at risk at 12 months.

  2. False. Informative censoring can bias the estimate in either direction. If high-risk patients leave, survival is overestimated; if low-risk patients leave, survival is underestimated.

  3. False. The hazard is an instantaneous rate, not a probability. It is the limiting probability of an event in a short interval divided by the length of that interval, and it can be greater than 1.

  4. True, provided the analysis time was specified in advance and the censoring is not related to prognosis. Administrative censoring at a pre-specified calendar date is the usual example of independent censoring.

  5. True.

  6. True.. Study time is calendar time measured from the start of the study; patient time is measured from each patient’s own time origin. If a study closes on a fixed calendar date, a patient recruited late has less patient time at closure than a patient recruited early. Administrative censoring therefore gives different patient-time censoring times, even though all patients are censored on the same calendar date. This is usually independent censoring if the closing date was pre-specified and the recruitment date is unrelated to prognosis.

There is a small convention issue at an event time. The empirical definition \(P(T\ge t)\) gives an estimate of 1.000 at 11 months, because all 11 men have a survival time of at least 11 months. survfit() reports 0.909 at 11 months, because it lets the curve drop at the event time and reports the value after the drop. Between event times the two conventions agree, and at 12 months both give 0.909. The important practical point is to say whether a reported “survival at 12 months” includes deaths at exactly 12 months or not!

  1. The empirical curve is a step function that drops at the event times, and the ggsurvfit() version does the same but it starts from time 0 and it adds one extra segment in the end. (If you use a microscope, the difference in the convention at the event times might be visible as well.)
emp_pulm |>

  geom_step(direction = "vh") +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 1)) +
  scale_x_continuous(limits = c(0, NA)) +
  survfit2(Surv(time) ~ 1, pulmonary) |>
    ggsurvfit() +
  scale_y_continuous(limits = c(0, 1))

Solution 5

(Lecture 1, slides 18 and 25: skewed survival times and the median survival time.)

Survival times are usually right-skewed and the mean is sensitive to a few long survivors; the median is the time by which half the patients have died and is more robust. Here the mean is slightly larger than the median, which is consistent with a right tail. If the largest observation were censored, the mean would not be estimable without an assumption about the unobserved tail; treating the censored time as a death would underestimate the mean. The median can still be estimated if the curve reaches 0.5.

Solution 6

Collett discusses this in Section 2.1.2.

The censored observation should be counted after the deaths at 13 months: the risk set just before that time includes everyone still under observation at that time, including the person who is censored there. The deaths are then removed, and the censored observation is removed afterwards. If the censoring were counted before the deaths, the risk set would be too small and the estimated survival would change.

Note that the time scale here is in months. If exact dates were available, the censoring could be counted before or after the deaths depending on the exact times of the events. Then the same convention would instead be used for deaths and censoring at the same number of days since time 0.

  1. At the last death time there is one person at risk and that person dies, so \(\hat S(t)=0\). Greenwood’s sum contains the term \(d/(n(n-d))=1/(1\times 0)=\infty\), and \(0\times\infty\) is NaN. The estimate is zero because the only remaining person died, but that does not mean the survival probability is known with certainty; the sample is small and the tail is poorly estimated. This is one reason why the last part of a Kaplan–Meier curve is fragile.

Solution 9

  1. The largest observed time is for:
slice_max(iud, time)
#>   time status
#> 1  107      1
#> 2  107      0
#> 3  107      0

The Kaplan–Meier estimate stays constant from week 97, until just before week 107. It is not defined after 107 weeks, because there is no one left at risk after the event and the censorings at week 107.

  1. If all observations at week 107 had been censored, there would be no event, so the estimate would remain from week 97, and it would still be undefined after week 107. If the only observation at week 107 had been an event with no other person at risk at that time, the factor would be \((1-1)/1=0\) and the curve would drop to zero at week 107.

  2. Beyond the largest observed time there is no information about the event rate; no one is under observation. Continuing the curve flat would assume that no events occur after the end of follow-up, which is an extra assumption, not a result of the data. The curve therefore stops at the last observed time.

Solution 12

(This is Lecture 2 material: the hazard \(h(t)\) is the instantaneous event rate at time \(t\) — the probability of an event in a short interval given no event before \(t\), divided by the interval length — and the cumulative hazard \(H(t)=\int_0^t h(u)\,du\) sums it up.)

# Kaplan–Meier
km <- survfit(
  Surv(time, status) ~ 1,
  data = sta310r::myeloma,
  stype = 1, # "direct" = "Kaplan-Meier" = "product-limit"
  ctype = 1

# exp(-Nelson–Aalen)
na_surv <- survfit(
  Surv(time, status) ~ 1,
  data = sta310r::myeloma,
  stype = 2, # exp(cumulative hazard) where cumhaz = Nelson–Aalen from ctype = 1
  ctype = 1
)

bind_rows(km = broom::tidy(km), na = broom::tidy(na_surv), .id = "estimator") |>
  ggplot(aes(time, estimate, color = estimator)) +
  geom_step(direction = "hv")

The Nelson–Aalen survivor estimate is larger than the Kaplan–Meier estimate!

  1. The slope of the cumulative hazard curve is the hazard: a steeper slope means a higher event rate.

Solution 15

bind_rows(
  unstratified = survdiff(Surv(time, status) ~ treatment, melanoma) |>
    broom::glance(),
  stratified = survdiff(
    Surv(time, status) ~ treatment + strata(age),
    data = melanoma
  ) |>
    broom::glance(),

) |>
  knitr::kable(digits = 3)

unstratified 0.756 1 0.385
stratified 0.688 1 0.407

  1. The stratified test compares the treatments within each age stratum and combines the observed-minus-expected differences and their variances across strata. It estimates a common treatment effect while allowing each age group to have its own baseline hazard. It assumes that the treatment effect is the same in each age stratum (no treatment-by-age interaction) and that the strata are comparable in the sense required by the test.

  2. The age distribution is fairly balanced between the treatment groups:

  1. Stratification can change the conclusion when age (or another stratifying factor) is strongly prognostic and the treatment groups differ substantially in its distribution. For example, if older patients were much more likely to receive one treatment and older age strongly increased mortality, the unstratified test could attribute the age effect to treatment. A stratified test would remove that confounding. If the treatment effect itself differs across strata, a common-effect stratified test may be misleading, and stratum-specific estimates or an interaction test would be needed.
